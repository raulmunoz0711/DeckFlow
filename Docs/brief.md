# DeckFlow

1. Who did you talk to, and are they the one who pays?
 “I talked directly with Dan, the owner of Game Citi TCG near Cal State Fullerton. Since he’s the owner, he’s involved in the day-to-day business and would be the person deciding whether a solution like this is useful and worth paying for.”
STORE WEBSITE: https://www.gamecititcg.com/

2. What did they say the problem is, in their words? When did it last happen?
 “Dan told me one of their problems is dealing with bulk trading cards. When they receive large amounts of bulk, employees have to manually go through the cards, sort them into their different categories, and organize them. He said it’s a repetitive process that takes a lot of employee time, and it’s something they deal with regularly when they receive bulk cards.”

3. What does it cost them? Give the number. What forces them to act?
“Dan estimated that they spend around 5–10 employee-hours per week sorting bulk. At roughly $20 an hour in labor, that could cost around $100–$200 a week, or several thousand dollars over a year. There’s also an opportunity cost because those employees could instead be helping customers, processing sales, listing inventory, or doing other work for the store.”

4. What have they already tried or paid for? Why aren’t they using what exists?
 “Right now, Dan said they mainly handle the process manually with employees. There are existing automated card sorters like GlideSorter and Roca, but they’re relatively expensive and may offer more than a smaller local card shop needs. That gives us an opportunity to investigate whether we could create something smaller, more affordable, and configurable specifically around Game Citi’s workflow.”

5. How big is the domain, who already sells this, and what does it cost?
 “The domain is automated trading-card sorting and inventory processing. There are already companies in this space, including GlideSorter and Roca, so we know there is an existing market for solving this problem. GlideSorter, for example, is very similar to our concept because it automatically feeds, identifies, and sorts cards into different outputs. Our focus would be whether we can create a more affordable solution appropriate for a smaller local card shop like Game Citi.”

6. What is hard about it that someone with AI couldn’t do in a weekend, and can your team do it in a year?
 “The difficult part isn’t just making an app. We’d have to build a complete hardware and software system that takes a stack of maybe 50 or more cards, feeds exactly one card at a time without damaging it, scans and identifies the card, determines where it belongs, and physically routes it into one of several configurable output bins. We’d also have to deal with computer vision, motors and sensors, double-feeds, jams, unknown cards, recognition errors, and keeping the software synchronized with the physical machine. AI can help us develop parts of it, but it can’t solve all of those engineering and integration problems over a weekend. A working prototype is something we believe could realistically be developed and tested over the course of a year.”
7. What do you still not know?
 “We still need to determine exactly how fast and accurate the machine would need to be for Dan to actually use it, how many output bins would make sense, what sorting options are most important to Game Citi, and what price would make it worthwhile for the business. We also don’t know yet which feeding and routing mechanisms will work most reliably without damaging cards, so those are things we’d need to research and prototype.”
